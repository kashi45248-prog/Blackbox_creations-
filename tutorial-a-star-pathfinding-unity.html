A* Pathfinding in Unity Without NavMesh Baking

Unity's NavMesh is great — until it isn't. 2D games, tile-based worlds, procedurally generated levels, and obstacles that move at runtime all fight against baked navigation. A* grid pathfinding is the answer. Here's how it works.

Where NavMesh struggles

NavMesh requires baking navigation data ahead of time. That breaks down when your world changes at runtime: destructible terrain, player-built walls, procedural dungeons, or anything in 2D (NavMesh is fundamentally a 3D system). You can rebake at runtime, but it's expensive and complex. For these games, you need pathfinding that thinks in grids, not baked meshes.

How A* works (the 30-second version)

A* (A-star) searches a grid of walkable cells from start to goal, always expanding the most promising cell first — the one with the lowest combined cost of "distance traveled so far" plus "estimated distance to goal." That heuristic is what makes it fast: unlike brute-force search, A* heads toward the target instead of wandering. The result is the shortest walkable path, even around complex obstacles.

Grid pathfinding in practice

A practical A* setup has four pieces:

  1. A grid over your world — cells marked walkable or blocked, in 2D or 3D.
  2. Agents that request paths from A to B.
  3. Obstacles that mark cells unwalkable — ideally updatable at runtime.
  4. A simple API — "give me the waypoints from here to there" — so your game code stays clean.

The grid approach shines for tower defense, RTS, roguelikes, puzzle games, and any tile-based design — genres where NavMesh was never the right tool.

Performance notes

A* on large grids with many agents can get expensive — keep your grid resolution as coarse as your gameplay allows, cache paths instead of recomputing every frame, and stagger path requests across frames for crowds. A well-tuned grid solver handles hundreds of agents without breaking a sweat.
